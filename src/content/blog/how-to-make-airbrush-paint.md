How to make airbrush paint is mostly a question of what you start with. You don't cook paint from scratch: you take an acrylic you already own, reduce it with airbrush medium until it flows like milk, strain it, and test spray. Get those four steps right and almost any decent acrylic will go through an airbrush.

**Quick answer:** Mix your acrylic with an airbrush medium (Golden and Liquitex both make one), add water first if the paint is thick tube paint, strain the mix through a nylon stocking or fine mesh, and adjust until it sprays clean at your normal pressure. Fluid acrylics and acrylic inks are the easiest starting point. Thick craft paint is the hardest.

---

## What Makes Airbrush Paint Different From Regular Acrylic Paint

Airbrush paint and brush paint are the same kind of product: [pigment](/airbrush-glossary/pigment/) held in an acrylic [binder](/airbrush-glossary/binder/). Three things make the airbrush version different.

- **Finer pigment.** The pigment is ground finer so it passes through a 0.2–0.5mm nozzle without building up at the tip. Coarse pigment is why some cheap paints clog no matter how much you thin them.
- **Lower viscosity, same binder.** The paint is thin enough to atomize, but it still carries enough binder to stick and form a proper film. That balance is the whole trick, and it's why plain water alone is not the answer.
- **Additives.** Many airbrush paints include a [retarder](/airbrush-glossary/retarder/) or [flow improver](/airbrush-glossary/flow-improver/) so the paint doesn't dry on the needle tip mid-session.

When you make your own airbrush paint, you are recreating those three properties: you control the viscosity with medium and water, you control the pigment problem by straining, and you add flow improver if tip dry becomes an issue.

---

## Why Airbrush Medium Beats Plain Water

Water thins paint, but it adds no binder. Thin a tube acrylic heavily with water alone and the binder gets spread so thin that the dry coat turns weak, chalky, and easy to lift with masking tape. Golden says this directly in its own notes: high water additions make the paint "fairly weak".

An airbrush medium is essentially binder without pigment, already reduced to a sprayable consistency. It thins the paint and tops up the binder at the same time. Liquitex also recommends its medium over plain water for thinning its paints for the airbrush.

Water still has a place. For thick tube paint, Golden's method adds water first to get the paint pourable, and then the medium. Small amounts are fine. Large amounts are the problem.

## How to Make Airbrush Paint, by Starting Paint

The ratios below come from the paint makers' own charts. They are starting points, not final answers: pigment size and concentration vary from colour to colour, and your needle size and pressure change what works. Golden notes its chart is written for 40 PSI or more, so if you spray lower, expect to thin further.

### Fluid Acrylics and Acrylic Inks: The Easy Route

These are already thin and finely ground, so they need the least work.

- **Golden Fluid Acrylics:** 1 part Airbrush Medium to 1 part paint, no water.
- **Liquitex Soft Body or Acrylic Ink:** start at 1:1 with Liquitex Airbrush Medium and adjust per colour.

Mix well, strain if you see any specks, and spray.

### Heavy Body Tube Acrylics: Water First, Then Medium

Heavy body paint is far too thick to spray straight from the tube. Golden's chart for Heavy Body is **1 part paint, 3 parts water, 4 parts Airbrush Medium**. Add the water first and stir until the paint is pourable, then add the medium.

Liquitex says to strain once mixed when you use its medium with Heavy Body, to catch undissolved paint. I'd strain every time with tube paint anyway.

### Craft Acrylics: Possible, With Limits

Cheap craft acrylics are the hardest to convert. They often use coarser pigment and fillers, so even a perfectly thinned mix can clog a fine nozzle.

If you want to try:

1. Thin with airbrush medium plus a little water until it flows like whole milk.
2. Strain it, then strain it again.
3. Use a 0.35mm needle or larger. A 0.2mm will fight you.
4. Expect to flush the nozzle more often than with real airbrush paint.

Craft paint is fine for practice, stencils and cheap projects. For anything you care about, a proper airbrush paint or a fluid acrylic is less frustrating.

### Pigment Powder: Advanced, Not for a First Try

You can make paint from dry pigment and an acrylic binder, but this is where homemade airbrush paint goes wrong most often. Loose pigment powders vary hugely in particle size, and grinding them fine enough for an airbrush nozzle takes a muller and patience. I haven't found a reliable published recipe for it, and I won't invent one.

If you want custom colours, the easier route is to mix them from fluid acrylics, which are already finely ground, and then reduce the mix with medium as above.

## Strain, Test, Adjust

Whatever you start with, finish the same way:

1. **Strain.** Golden suggests cheesecloth, nylon pantyhose or a #8 mesh or finer. Disposable [paint strainers](/go/paint-strainers) do the same job with less mess.
2. **Check the consistency.** You are aiming for whole milk. My [thinning guide](/blog/how-to-thin-acrylic-paint-for-airbrush/#the-milk-test-classic) covers the milk test, the needle line test and the drop test in detail.
3. **Test spray on scrap.** Fine, even lines at your normal pressure mean you're done. Spidering means too thin. Spitting, a rough texture or tip build-up means too thick or not strained enough.
4. **Add flow improver if the tip keeps drying.** A drop or two is usually enough.

Match the needle to the paint. Homemade mixes from tube or craft paint are happier in a 0.35mm or larger needle; see my [needle size guide](/blog/airbrush-needle-sizes/) for the details.

Want to skip the medium and mix your own thinner instead? The [DIY thinner recipe](/blog/how-to-thin-acrylic-paint-for-airbrush/#diy-thinner-recipe) is in the thinning guide, along with brand-by-brand ratios.

## Colours You Should Never Spray

Spraying turns paint into a fine mist you can breathe in, which changes the safety picture compared with brushing. Golden's own guidance is not to spray genuine cadmium pigments or any colour containing zinc white, and it lists Manganese Blue Hue, Viridian Green Hue and Zinc White among its Fluid Acrylics as colours that should never be sprayed.

Check the label or the maker's data sheet before you convert any artist paint, and wear a respirator rated for paint mist whenever you airbrush, water-based paint included.

## Cleaning Up

Homemade airbrush paint dries like any acrylic, so don't let it sit in the airbrush. Flush with water while the paint is still wet, then run an airbrush cleaner through. Golden notes that water, an ammonia and water mix, alcohol or household cleaners all work, but check what your cleaner does to the airbrush seals before you use it regularly. The [airbrush cleaning guide](/blog/how-to-clean-airbrush/) has my full routine.
